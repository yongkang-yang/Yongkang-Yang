# Judge the Outcome, Not the Tool

Nobody asks a novelist to write a manuscript by hand to prove that it counts. We read the book, not the handwriting.

Why should AI-assisted work be different? If the result is better and the work gets done faster, that matters more to me than how many steps a person completed unaided.

A language model can supply the argument as well as the words. So I want to know whether the argument holds, not how many sentences the author typed.

The distinction that matters is between learning a skill and getting a job done.

A [recent experimental study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04721v5), *AI Assistance Reduces Persistence and Hurts Independent Performance*, illustrates the distinction. Across three randomized experiments involving 1,222 recruited participants, AI assistance improved immediate performance on mathematical reasoning and reading comprehension tasks. After the assistance was withdrawn, previously assisted participants performed worse and skipped more problems than participants who had worked without AI.

A high score with AI is not proof of learning. Remove the tool and see what someone can do on their own. But when the task is to produce a report, write code or make an argument, judge the finished work. Is it accurate? Does it work? Does it hold up?

Lauren Tan, who leads Grok Bot engineering, [suggested](https://x.com/poteto/status/2108689504811012135) two rounds for software-engineering interviews: explain a system design, then build something real with AI agents. Can you think through a problem? Can you turn an idea into working software? These questions tell us more than another LeetCode puzzle.

The person who submits the work still owns it, including the mistakes. A broken program is still broken, however it was written.

For learning, demand understanding. For delivery, demand quality. Judge the outcome, not the tool.
