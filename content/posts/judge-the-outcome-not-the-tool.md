# Judge the Outcome, Not the Tool

Nobody asks a novelist to rewrite a manuscript by hand to prove that the writing counts. We care about the work: whether it is insightful, convincing and worth reading. A computer changes how an author produces a text; it does not, by itself, invalidate the result.

AI-assisted work should face the same basic test. If assistance makes a task faster and the outcome better, why reject it simply because a human did not perform every step unaided?

The objection is that AI does more than replace a pen. It can supply reasoning, arguments and answers. That difference deserves scrutiny, but it does not settle the question. We still need to ask whether the result is correct, whether it serves its purpose and whether someone can take responsibility for it. The origin of an answer is not a substitute for evaluating its quality.

I think much of the debate becomes confused because two different purposes are treated as one: learning to do something and getting something done.

A [recent experimental study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04721v5), *AI Assistance Reduces Persistence and Hurts Independent Performance*, illustrates the distinction. Across three randomized experiments involving 1,222 recruited participants, AI assistance improved immediate performance on mathematical reasoning and reading comprehension tasks. After the assistance was withdrawn, previously assisted participants performed worse and skipped more problems than participants who had worked without AI.

That finding matters for a specific reason: a higher assisted score does not prove independent learning. It says little about the value of an AI-assisted final product, and it does not establish lasting cognitive harm.

Learning and delivery require different tests. To assess learning, remove the assistance and see whether someone can apply the skill. To assess delivery, examine the work: Is the analysis sound? Does the program work? Does the argument hold? In each case, the outcome—not the method—sets the standard.

Lauren Tan, who leads Grok Bot engineering, [proposed](https://x.com/poteto/status/2108689504811012135) two technical interview rounds: explain a system design, then build a real project with AI agents. The first tests engineering judgment; the second tests whether a candidate can turn an idea into a high-quality result. It is a proposal, not an announced company policy—but it captures the point: assess what people can deliver with the tools available.

Responsibility remains. Claims and calculations need checking, and rules on confidentiality, disclosure and authorship still apply. But requiring unaided work simply because it is unaided mistakes the process for the purpose.

Pens, computers and language models are different tools. The point is not to treat them as identical. For learning, demand understanding. For delivery, demand quality. Judge the outcome, not the tool.
